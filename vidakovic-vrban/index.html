Two follow-up checks prompted by Robertina's account

Two things in Robertina's message were worth checking against outside sources rather than taking at face value: whether a birth register (as opposed to the marriage registers already read) exists anywhere online, and whether Jakov's and Marko's deaths are independently documented. Both were checked directly.

No birth register exists onlineThe same archive.org uploader ("arhivgen@byom.de") who published the four marriage registers has uploaded nothing else for this parish cluster — confirmed directly via archive.org's search API, which returns exactly those four items and no others. A broader search across all of archive.org for a Sveti Rok, Lovinac, Gračac, or Ričice birth, baptism, or death register turned up nothing. The death book the earlier Find a Grave transcriptions draw on (citing page and entry numbers) is real, but exists only as a physical book at the civil registry office in Gračac — not digitised anywhere online.
Jakov's and Marko's deaths line up with a documented historical event, but neither is individually recorded in the one database checkedThe killings Robertina describes — Jakov "at his own doorstep," Marko "taken from the house" — fall squarely inside the Serb/Chetnik uprising of late July–August 1941, which devastated exactly this area: Sveti Rok's own deanery (Udbinski dekanat, covering Sveti Rok, Lovinac, Gračac, Ričice, Bunić, Udbina, Palanka, Rudopolje, and Boričevac) went from roughly 16,000 Catholics before the war to under 2,000 after it, with several of those parishes wiped out entirely. This isn't a guess tied to this family specifically — it's the well-documented regional catastrophe their flight across Velebit was part of. Checked directly against the Muzej žrtava genocida's searchable national WWII victims database (659,122 entries, built from the 1964 Yugoslav government census and later revisions) — neither Jakov Vidaković nor Marko Vrban appears anywhere in it, by name or by any combination of name and place. That database does record 33 named war victims specifically from Sveti Rok (place field "Rok"), so the village itself isn't absent from it — including two Miletić entries, Kata Miletić (father Jakov, born 1890) and Jure Miletić (father Stipe, born 1925), which given Marija Miletić's own 1890 birth year are worth watching as possible relatives, though not confirmed as such. The census's own documentation admits it's incomplete and specifically that "victims from the other side... were not even recorded" and that postwar migration meant many families weren't around to report their dead — which fits a family that had already fled to Slavonski Brod by the time anyone was collecting names.
Two same-surname false leads, worth naming so they aren't mistaken for a connection later A general web search for "Vidaković" and "Vrban" together with wartime Lika turned up two unrelated people who happen to share these surnames: an Ante Vrban of Senj (father Jozo, born 1924 per the same victims database — a perpetrator, not a victim, associated with wartime killings at Jadovno) and a Nikica Vidaković connected to a 1941 massacre at Glina, over 150 km away. Neither has any documented tie to this family — different birthplace, different generation, different event — and both are flagged here only so a future search doesn't mistake a shared surname for a shared family.

The Slavonski Brod grave, confirmed directly — and a live, tended photo

Everything so far has focused on Sveti Rok, where the family came from. But Ante and Franjka lived out their lives and are buried in Slavonski Brod — a modern city with its own online cemetery records, which turned out to be exactly the kind of source the Lika research kept running into a wall on. Slavonski Brod's municipal utility runs a public cemetery lookup (e-Groblje Slavonski Brod), and it has both of them.

Confirmed, exact matchSearching "Gradsko groblje" (the city cemetery) for Vidaković returns Antun Vidaković — Ante's full baptismal name — died 10.09.1991, buried 11.09.1991; and Franjka Vidaković, died 05.05.2003, buried 07.05.2003. Both dates match Robertina's account exactly. The plot reference given — Starokatoličko, P:8 – R:07 – G:01 — is character-for-character the same plot code already on the memorial card at the top of this page, confirming the two records are the same grave.
The grave is still tendedThe system includes a site photo dated 11 October 2022 showing the headstone with fresh flowers and a watering can at the base — the same monument as the memorial card photo, but recent. Someone was visiting and maintaining this grave as of 2022, which means there is very likely a living relative reachable through Slavonski Brod itself, independent of Robertina's own line.
Two possible relatives in the same city, neither confirmedThe same lookup turns up a Nikola Vidaković (died 2016) and a Manda Milošević (died 2018) in Slavonski Brod — the same first names as two of Ante's siblings Robertina named. Neither is confirmed: Nikola's plot holds unrelated surnames (Šaf, Pavičić) with no link to Ante or Jakov, and — if he really were Ante's brother — he would have been well over 100 at death, which is possible but worth real skepticism. Manda Milošević's plot has no co-buried Vidaković either. Both are noted here as candidates only, the same way candidates from the Sveti Rok registers are marked elsewhere on this page — not as matches.
Ivan Vidaković (the candidate child) — checked, not hereThe "candidate children" section below lists an Ivan Vidaković, 1931–2008, buried in Sveti Rok Cemetery, as a plausible son of Ante and Franjka. He does not appear in Slavonski Brod's Gradsko groblje records — the only three Ivan Vidakovićs there died in 1993, 1997, and 2015, none matching 2008. If he really is Ante and Franjka's son, he stayed in or returned to Sveti Rok rather than joining the family's move — which doesn't rule him out, but doesn't strengthen the case either.
The natural next step this opens up Slavonski Brod has its own matični ured (civil registry office) — Petra Krešimira IV br. 1, 35000 Slavonski Brod, tel. 035/216-111 — which would hold Ante's and Franjka's actual death certificates. Croatian death certificates routinely name the deceased's parents, so a certified copy of Ante's would very likely give Jakov Vidaković and Marija Miletić's names directly from a 1991 civil record — independent confirmation, and possibly resolving the odd 16-year age gap flagged above, without waiting on a 19th-century parish register at all. The cemetery office itself (TD Komunalac, uprava@komunalac-sb.hr) is also worth asking who currently holds the burial rights to plot P:8–R:07–G:01, since whoever is paying for its upkeep and bringing flowers in 2022 is almost certainly a traceable living relative.

Trying the same trick in Bizovac and Radovanje — a clean negative result

Slavonski Brod's e-Groblje worked because it's a city with a well-resourced municipal utility company behind it. The same approach was tried for Jeka's town (Bizovac) and Marija's (Radovanje, in the Oriovac municipality) — and came back empty, for a specific, explainable reason rather than a dead end.

BizovacNo dedicated digital cemetery system exists. The closest thing online, Nekros.info — a crowd-sourced Balkan-wide obituary site — lists both of Bizovac's cemeteries ("Groblje u Bizovcu" and "Groblje u Brođancima"), but only holds a handful of very recent, user-submitted obituaries (2019 onward) rather than a full historical ledger. No Dukić entry there. Bizovac is a small municipality (population under 4,000) run directly by the local council, with no equivalent of Slavonski Brod's Komunalac.
RadovanjeRadovanje is a hamlet of the Oriovac municipality — in the same county as Slavonski Brod, which raised hope its cemetery might be run by the same company. It isn't: Oriovac has its own small municipal utility, Orio-kom d.o.o., which handles burials and cemetery upkeep but has no public digital lookup of any kind.
What this means in practice For Jeka (Dukić) and Marija (Sporer), there's no substitute for the same kind of direct contact already used for Gospić and Slavonski Brod: Općina Bizovac (Kralja Tomislava 89, Bizovac — tel. +385 31 675 301 / 675 674, opcina-bizovac.hr) for Jeka, and Orio-kom d.o.o. (Trg Hrvatskog preporoda 1, Oriovac — tel. +385 35 456 050, oriokomdoo@gmail.com) for Marija. Both are small enough that a direct phone call asking whether a Dukić or Sporer grave exists in their cemetery records is likely faster than a written letter — unlike Gospić's archive, these are living municipal offices, not historical record-keepers.

Checking whether the siblings' marriages are hiding in the Lika registers after all

A fair question: the four marriage registers were read cover-to-cover looking for "Vidaković" and "Vrban," but Robertina's sibling names — Jeka, Kata, Manda — only came to light afterwards, married into Dukić, Modrić, and Milošević. Could one of those marriages be sitting in Sveti Rok or Lovinac's own register, filed under a groom's surname nobody was watching for at the time?

Two checks, both quick, neither requiring a re-read of the register images themselves:

The register's own OCR textDownloaded the full OCR text file archive.org generates for both books and searched it directly for Dukić, Modrić, Milošević, Sporer, Vidaković, and Vrban. Every one of the last two returned zero matches — proof the OCR is genuine noise on this handwriting, not a usable index, exactly as earlier sessions found. Not a real search; ruled out and moved on.
Why the answer was already sitting in the coverage logThe point of the original page-by-page read wasn't to find "Vidaković" specifically — it was to check every marriage entry in both books for either surname, bride's side or groom's. That log states plainly, for Lovinac: "read cover to cover... No Vidaković and no Vrban" anywhere in 265 pages. Sveti Rok's log says the same. Across all four registers combined (roughly 1,000 pages), exactly one Vidaković marriage entry exists at all — Tomo Vidaković × Marta Budak, already identified as an unrelated earlier-generation household, not a sibling. Since a Jeka, Kata, or Manda Vidaković marrying a Dukić, Modrić, or Milošević groom would necessarily have shown up as a Vidaković entry, and the log already establishes there is only that one, unrelated entry in the whole span — none of the three sibling marriages is in these two books. No new page-by-page reading needed to reach that.
What this actually confirms The three sisters' marriages happened either after 1940 (past where these registers stop) or in a different parish entirely — most likely the latter, and most likely tied to the same 1941 flight already documented on this page. If Jeka, Kata, and Manda were still unmarried when the family fled across Velebit, they would have married their Dukić, Modrić, and Milošević husbands only after arriving in Bizovac, Brdovec, and Radovanje — which is exactly why those marriages can't be found in a Lika parish register no matter how carefully it's read, and why the municipal contacts above are the only real path to them.

What the names themselves mean

Not genealogy, just background — where these names come from, and how common they are.

Vidaković is a South Slavic patronymic — "son of Vidak," a pet form of Vidoje or Vida, from the old Slavic root vid, "to see" or "to know." About 3,300 people carry it in Croatia today, mostly from the Senj area — the same county Sveti Rok sits in. Worth noting: one of the surname's largest modern concentrations is Slavonski Brod (120 people) — the same city the grave card names for Ante and Franjka's actual grave, which fits a family that moved there and simply carried the name with them. Croatian onomastics scholarship (Petar Šimunović) traces the surname's earliest documented appearance to the 13th century, in Kotlenice near Dugopolje, inland from Split — but that's the name's earliest known root anywhere, not a claim about this specific family. Patronymics like "son of Vidak" arose independently in different villages, and the name has several other, clearly unrelated branches on record (a noble line from the Bunjevac migration into Bačka, a separate one from Montenegro's Nikšić clan) — sharing a surname proves nothing on its own without a document connecting the generations. A handful of notable Vidakovićs, none known to connect to this family: Milovan Vidaković (1780–1841), a prominent early Serbian novelist; and Albe Vidaković (1914–1964), a Croatian composer, Catholic priest, and musicologist.

"Sošina" — the by-name attached to Ante's branch — fits a well-documented Lika naming pattern rather than being unique to this family: house or family nicknames there were commonly formed by adding the possessive suffix -in/-ina to an ancestor's personal name or nickname (so "Sošina" would read as "belonging to Šoso/Sošo's household"). These nicknames were never formally registered — they lived in speech, not in parish books — which is itself part of why they're hard to trace on paper.

Vrban comes from vrba, "willow." About 1,300 people carry it in Croatia today, historically concentrated around Pag, Primorje, Lika, and western Herzegovina — squarely the right region. First documented in Croatian parish records in 1691. One flag worth raising directly, since a search will surface it: there is a historical Ante Vrban (1908–1948) on Wikipedia — a Ustaše officer executed for war crimes after WWII. He is not connected to this family in any way found so far — different generation, different branch of a fairly common surname pairing, and nothing here places him in Sveti Rok. Named here only so it doesn't turn up as a nasty surprise on a later search.

Miletić — the surname behind "Miletić gaj" — is a patronymic from Mile or Mileta, itself a pet form of names like Milan or Milenko, built on the Slavic root mio/mil, "dear" or "beloved." Literally, then, "Miletić gaj" reads as something like "the beloved ones' grove."

The given names, for completeness: Ante is the Croatian form of Anthony (Latin Antonius); Franjka is the feminine of Franjo, Croatian for Francis (from Franciscus, "the Frenchman," ultimately "free" — and it recurs in this same family cluster: Tomo Vidaković's mother-in-law was a Franjica Budak, the same root name again); Jakov is Jacob/James (Hebrew, traditionally "supplanter" or "may God protect"); and Marija is Mary (Hebrew Miriam, meaning debated, often given as "beloved" or "wished-for child").
