Apollonio, later Champion — and the trail of "Campione"

A page started in answer to a call for help in a genealogy group: a family born in Rovigno in the 1820s, who emigrated to Brownsville, Texas and anglicized Apollonio to Champion — carrying a family nickname, Campione, now found exactly where the original post said it would be, in a Rovinj parish census record.

The family sheet itself is now identified — it's a page from Rovinj parish's Status Animarum ("stanje duša," a household-by-household census kept by the church) for house no. 833, headed by Giorgio Apollonio and his widow Maria Bronzin, certified as a true copy by the State Archive in Pazin. The "Campione" annotation the original post asked about is right where it should be, in the Annotazioni column next to Giorgio's death entry.

Still open The family history book (Campion(e) History, by Raul Guerra) describes this exact annotation plus two more: one on Simon Apollonio's own entry (Giorgio's son, Simone's grandson), and one on Pietro Apollonio's entry via his widow Gaspera Segalla. Only the Giorgio/Maria Bronzin one has been found so far — see "Where to look next," below, for the concrete lead on where the other two almost certainly are.

The original request

Posted to a genealogy group, asking for help locating one of the documents referenced in a family history book:

"Hi! I was wondering if anyone could help me with this search! Our ancestors were born in Rovigno Istria in the 1820s, and emigrated to Texas. In Istria, their last name was Apollonio but when they came to the states, they changed it to Champion. Supposedly they had a nickname of Campione. The family sheet references Campione in the upper right, see the photo. There is a family history book which vaguely states other places that the nickname Campione can be found, but I can't find the documents! Please see the typed images for info. Can anyone help me look for one of the documents mentioned that state the name Campione in the annotation? Or point me in the right direction? Thank you all so much!" — original post, as written

The book: Campion(e) History, by Raul Guerra

The typed pages from the family history book, page 128, explain exactly where the name comes from and how it became a surname:

"The South Texas surname CHAMPION was used by five brothers in America. The change from APOLLONIO to CHAMPION is as follows. In a letter to A. Champion dated 1994, the director of the Center of Historical Research in Rovigno, G. Radossi, sent information on the APOLLONIO family that he had in his archives. It included photo copies of the book entries pertaining to the APOLLONIO. In those documents it showed the Maria BRONZIN family, the widow of Giorgio APOLLONIO, who died on 02 Feb 1858, the son of the late Simone [APOLLONIO]. Note: the name APOLLONIO was also spelled APPOLONIO. On the margin of this document was noted "Campion". The same notation "Campion" was also in the margin of Simon APOLLONIO, the son of Giorgio and grandson of Simon. The next entry was for Gaspera Segalla the widow of Pietro APOLLONIO the son of Simon. Again the name "Campion" was attached to Pietro's record." — Raul Guerra, Campion(e) History, p. 128

And on where the nickname itself came from:

"Pietro APOLLONIO and Giorgio APOLLONIO were brothers, Giorgio being older by two years. In his letter, Mr Radossi stated that one of the CHAMPION ancestors had the nickname "Campion(e)" and therefore the brothers used that name in America. The name would be annotated as APOLLONIO ditto CAMPION(E). It appears that Simone had this nickname attached to his surname but it is not known where it originated and for what reason. It appears that an ancestor was a "champion" of something, what and when is unknown. […] After several generations of using the "ditto" or "dit" name the family could drop the surname and utilize the "ditto" or "dit" name exclusively and that name would then became the surname. This is what happened to the APOLLONIO ditto CAMPION(E) family when they moved to America." — Raul Guerra, Campion(e) History, p. 128
So three annotations exist, by the book's own account One on Giorgio Apollonio's own entry (found — see below). One on Simon Apollonio, Giorgio's son and Simone's grandson. One on Pietro Apollonio's entry, Giorgio's brother, reached via his widow Gaspera Segalla. The book doesn't say these three are on the same physical page — and the one that's been found isn't a single page, it's a parish census book organized by house number, so the other two are very likely a few pages away in the same set of volumes, under different house numbers.

Found: the document itself — house 833, Rovinj Status Animarum

This is a Stato delle Anime / Status Animarum page — a household census the parish kept, updated over years as children were born, married, emigrated, or died — for house no. 833 in Rovigno, certified by the Državni arhiv u Pazinu (State Archive in Pazin) as a true copy of the original.

Household headApollonio Giorgio, q. [fu, "the late"] Simone — born Rovigno, died 2 Feb 1858.
WifeBronzin Maria, q. Sebastiano, q. Giovanni — listed as his widow.
Sons listedSimone · Sebastiano Alberto · Pietro · Andrea (celibe, "unmarried" — residence given as Lima) · Nicolò · Giuseppe. Residence ("Dimora") for several of the sons is given as Brownsville — Texas, right in the record itself.
AnnotazioniTwo notes in the margin: a date (~1 Aug 1858) with "Campione" written directly beneath it, against Giorgio's death entry — the exact annotation the original post was looking for — and a second note further down reading "Vedova con [6] figli" ("widow, with 6 children").

* Albert, Peter, Andrew, Nicholas, and Joseph are the "five brothers" the family history book opens with. The book's second "Campione" annotation, on Pietro Apollonio son of Simon (Giorgio's brother, via his widow Gaspera Segalla), belongs to a different, earlier branch — Simon (b. 1749)'s son Pietro, not to be confused with Giorgio's own son Pietro/Peter Champion above.

The chart itself goes back one more generation — but the photo cuts it off Look closely at the supplied photo above and there's a further column of names on its right edge, physically cropped by the camera: Simon Appollonio's own parents (his father's name is cut entirely; his mother appears only as "Fransisca... B-Rovign[o]"), Domenica Nider's parents ("Piero Nider" and "Santa Sb...," both cut off), and, one column further right again, the parents of Bastian Bronzin ("Andrea B[ronzin]" and "Catareina...") and of Zuanna Cossovel ("Santo Cri..." and a mother not visible at all). None of this is guesswork we can complete from this photo — the names are genuinely outside the frame, not just small. The single most useful thing that could be sent for this page is a fuller or rescrolled photo of this same chart, wide enough to catch that right-hand column in full.
What's been checked on the Bronzin, Cossovel, and Nider names specifically: Geni.com has profiles for Giorgio, Maria Bronzin, and their children (sourced from an Ancestry tree, ID 111701831, managed by Geoffrey David Trowbridge) — but nothing yet for Bastian Bronzin, Zuanna Cossovel, or Piero Nider individually. FamilySearch's own record search returns zero indexed hits for "Cossovel" anywhere in the world, and zero for "Bronzin" born in Rovigno — because, as established earlier, the actual Rovinj parish registers on FamilySearch (Rođeni 1751–1815 would cover Bastian's 1755 birth) are camera-digitized but not name-indexed; they can only be searched by opening each film and reading page by page. FindMyPast has no Rovigno/Istria church-record coverage at all — a "Bronzin" search there returns 141 results, all unrelated U.S. immigrant families. The Bronzin and Cossovel lines are real people, named twice over (the chart and the Status Animarum in-law reference), but tracing them further back is only possible by browsing the unindexed Rovinj birth/marriage films directly, or from a clearer copy of the chart itself.

Found: the brothers themselves, in Texas

U.S. census records match the Rovigno chart closely enough to remove any doubt these are the same men — and add the Gulf-port arrival record the roadmap only hoped might exist.

The 1850 arrivalA passenger index entry for "L Champion," age 33, born Italy 1817, arriving in the United States in 1850 aboard the ship Louisiana — United States, Index to Passenger Arrivals, Atlantic and Gulf Ports, 1820–1874 (a supplemental index covering Gulf and Atlantic ports excluding New York). The initial is almost certainly a misread "A" for Albert (b. 1816) — the age and birth year match him more closely than any other brother. This confirms the Gulf-coast arrival route the roadmap proposed, rather than a direct Texas landing. (FamilySearch record)
1850 census — Webb, Texas (Rio Grande Valley)Albert Champion (32, Italy), Peter Champion (b. 1820, Italy), and Nicolas Champion (b. 1823, Italy) all already resident here — the earliest U.S. record of any of the brothers, within a year of the "Louisiana" arrival above. Albert's household includes Jose Ma Solis, Estefana Solis, and Josefa Champion (age 20, Mexico) — Albert's Mexican in-laws and, it appears, his wife. (Albert, Peter, Nicolas)
1860 census — Point Isabel, Cameron Co.Albert, Peter, Joseph (b. 1829), Nicolas (35, with wife Teresa, Mexico, and children Simon/Nicolas/Catharine/ Sebastian all born in Texas), and a George Champion (20, born Austria ~1840) living in Nicolas's household — too young to be one of the five brothers, and not yet placed on the chart. Worth treating as an open question rather than assuming who he is. (Albert, Peter, Joseph, Nicolas & household, George)

Andrew — not among themNo Andrew/Andrea Champion turns up in any Cameron County census — and a published family account (below) explains why in more specific terms than this page first guessed: not "Lima," but the 1849 California Gold Rush, reached by ship to Panama. He was never heard from again.

All four Texas brothers married women from Mexico and raised large bilingual families on the border — which is worth noting on its own: within one generation, an Istrian "detto Campione" household became a Rio Grande Valley Tejano one.

Found: a published biography, and a family that already did this research

Two more sources close most of what was still open about the Texas side — one an authoritative published biography, the other a family member who has spent over a decade doing exactly this kind of work.

The Handbook of Texas entryThe Texas State Historical Association has a full sourced biography, "Champion, Albert", written by "A. A. Champion" — almost certainly a descendant, and quite possibly connected to the "A. Champion" who received Radossi's 1994 letter in the first place. It confirms Albert spent "several years at sea" before settling in Mobile, Alabama in the early 1840s, then moved to New Orleans, where Peter and Nicholas joined him — the Gulf-route mechanism the roadmap only guessed at, now stated outright. The brothers ran sail lighters at the founding of Point Isabel, Albert later ran the U.S. Mail Stage Line (1858–73) and two ranches (La Florida, the oldest in Cameron County, and La Gloria), married Estéfana Solís in 1850, and was appointed pilot commissioner for the Brazos Santiago port in 1873. He died in Brownsville on 20 Sep 1890. Its bibliography cites the Brownsville Herald (17 Jan 1937), the Gilbert D. Kingsbury Papers (Briscoe Center, UT Austin), and the Joseph Kleiber Letters (UT Brownsville) — three named primary sources not yet checked directly.
A family history project already existsA Find a Grave contributor named "Rovigno Champions" — active for 15+ years, 494 memorials added — maintains 19 virtual cemeteries for this exact family, including "Original Champion Brothers and Wives" (9 memorials) and "Champion Family of South Texas" (412 memorials). This is very likely the single best living contact for anything still open on this page — including the two missing "Campione" annotations back in Rovigno.

What their records add

Deaths and burials, confirmedAlbert d. 20 Sep 1890, Old City Cemetery, Brownsville (Champion Vault, Blk 11 Lot 26) · Peter — recorded here as "Pietro Campeoni Champion" — d. 20 May 1898, same cemetery (Blk 60 Lot 01) · Nicolas d. 1889, Our Lady of Mercy Catholic Cemetery, Mercedes · Joseph ("Jose," carrying his mother's name as a middle name, Joseph Bronzin Champion) d. 26 Mar 1882, Port Isabel City Cemetery. "Campeoni" and "Bronzin" surviving as recorded middle/alternate names is itself a small, independent echo of the "Campione" nickname trail.
Three Solís sistersAlbert married Estéfana Solís, Peter married Maria Felicitas Solís, and Nicolas married Teresa Solís — all three daughters of Francisco Solís and Anastacia Rivas. Joseph married Marcela Garcia in 1861. A fourth Solís woman, Cirilda Solís Champion (1843–1919), also appears among the "original wives" — not yet placed, but plausibly a second marriage after Marcela's 1880 death.
Only four brothers, not fiveBoth Albert's and Joseph's memorials state plainly: "Four of the six [sons] immigrated from Italy to Texas... Andrew (1822–?)" has no memorial and no death date recorded by the family itself — independent confirmation, from a completely different source than the Rovigno register, that Andrew did not come to Texas.
Direct evidence of the maritime connectionJoseph's own U.S. census entries record his occupation as sailor (1860), seaman (1870), then stock raiser (1880) — the Rovigno-to-Gulf-coast maritime link the roadmap proposed, now documented directly rather than inferred.
A discrepancy — now resolvedFind a Grave gives Giorgio's death year as 1850 ("Giorgio Campeoni, 1783–1850"), against the 2 Feb 1858 on the certified Status Animarum copy this page holds. A public Geni.com profile for Giorgio — sourced from an Ancestry.com tree (ID 111701831, managed by Geoffrey David Trowbridge, another possible contact) — independently gives his death as 1858, matching this page's own certified document. Two independent sources now agree against Find a Grave's one; 1850 looks like the actual typo. The same Geni profile gives Simon Appollonio's fuller name as "Simone Appollonio DeChampione" — the epithet already fused onto his name as a surname-in-practice a generation before the book's own account of the brothers adopting it formally in Texas.
George, fully resolvedA logged-in Geni profile settles it: George Champion, b. 24 Aug 1840, Rovigno, d. 1873, was Simon Apollonio's own son (by his second wife, Mattea Segariol) — Giorgio's grandson, not his son. Simon — the brother who supposedly "stayed in Rovigno" — in fact had at least three sons of his own who carried the Champion name: George, Antonio, and Giuseppe. George himself emigrated and is exactly the 20-year-old found in uncle Nicolas's 1860 household; he later married Cirila and had five children (Virginia, Albert, Cirilda, Josefina, Louis), all with their own Find a Grave memorials in the "Rovigno Champions" project. This is a genuinely different George from Albert's own son George Champion Sr (1852–1929) — two cousins sharing a name, both real.
Peter and Nicolas, individuallyPeter's memorial gives his marriage to Felicitas Solís as 7 Dec 1856 (not 1850) and lists all twelve children, including a Timoteo (b. 1858) matching the child seen in the 1880 census. Nicolas's memorial confirms his marriage to Teresa Solís in 1854 and his death in Santa Maria, Cameron Co., in 1889.
"Four" or "five" — the family's own records disagreeAlbert's, Joseph's, and Nicolas's memorials all say "four of the six [sons] immigrated... to Texas" — but Peter's says "five of their six children immigrated to Texas." This is a real inconsistency inside the family's own Find a Grave project, not just between it and the Rovigno register — worth asking "Rovigno Champions" directly whether Andrew is now believed to have reached Texas after all, or whether this is simply a copy-paste slip between memorials.
A possible living descendantA commenter named Frances Skipworth left messages on Albert's memorial calling him "great, great grandpa," and on Joseph's and Nicolas's calling them "great, great uncle" — consistent with being a direct descendant of Albert specifically. Worth a polite Find a Grave message alongside the one to "Rovigno Champions" above, since she may be a different person with her own knowledge of the family.

Found: a town founder, a landmark building, and a museum in the family's own store

Joseph's own son turns out to have a Handbook of Texas entry of his own — and the building he built in 1899 is still standing, and still open to the public.

Handbook of Texas: "Champion, Charles"Charles Champion: Pioneer Merchant and Developer of Port Isabel, Texas, by Norman Rozeff, confirms Charles (1870–1926) as Joseph's son (here "Joseph Bronson Champion") and Marcela Garcia's — matching "Charles A. 'Carlos' Champion I" already found via Find a Grave. Charles and his brother John clerked in Point Isabel's Hess General Store as young men; John bought half of it by 1887, and Charles bought the rest in 1894, renaming it "The Champion Store." In 1899 he built a two-story brick building, "The Key of the Gulf," which held the store, the U.S. Customs House, and the post office — Charles served as Point Isabel's second postmaster. He later co-owned the Santa Isabel land grant itself (which contains Point Isabel) with Judge James B. Wells, Jr., married Margarita Orive in 1897, and died in 1926, buried in Port Isabel City Cemetery — on land he had personally donated to the Catholic Church as the town cemetery.
The building still stands — as a museumThe 1899 Champion building is now the Port Isabel Historical Museum, 317 E. Railroad Ave., Port Isabel — still trading under the family name in spirit, with the original fish mural (painted 1906) still on its façade. The museum's own page adds one more precise detail not seen elsewhere: Joseph Champion "settled in Texas in 1855." A Texas Historical Commission marker for Charles Champion stands at the same address.
A published book to track downThe Handbook entry's bibliography cites Charles Champion, Point Isabel Pioneer: His Family and His Life, 1870–1926 (Laguna Madre Museum Foundation and the Port Isabel Historical Museum, 1999) — a real, dedicated, published family history book, unlike the private "Campion(e) History" pamphlet. Not yet found in any online catalog; the museum itself is the obvious place to ask whether it still has copies.

Found: the origin story, told by a great-grandson

A full open-access volume from UT Rio Grande Valley's regional history series turns out to contain a detailed, sourced account of exactly this family's arrival — written by a direct descendant. This settles most of what "Where to look next" (below) was still guessing at.

The sourceFrank Champion Murphy, "Don Juan Jose de Solis, Albert Champion, the Border Cattle Raids, and the Birth of the Cattle Kingdom," in Additional Studies in Rio Grande Valley History (Brownsville: University of Texas at Brownsville and Texas Southmost College, 2008), pp. 148–185+. The full volume is freely downloadable from ScholarWorks @ UTRGV. The author states plainly that Albert and Estéfana Solís "would be my great-grandparents" — Frank Champion Murphy is himself part of this family, not an outside historian.
Why Austria, and why the seaThe brothers' motive for leaving wasn't opportunity — it was conscription. The Austrian Empire annexed the Adriatic coast at the Congress of Vienna in 1815; each brother, as he came of age under Habsburg rule, "went to sea... to avoid conscription by the hated Austrian army" rather than serve. That is the real root of the maritime connection this page's roadmap only guessed at.
The actual mechanism: hauling war suppliesBy 1846 all four — Albert (32), Peter (30), Andrew (26), Nicholas (23) — had independently ended up in New Orleans after years sailing "the stately sailships of the period." When the U.S. Army massed supply ships at New Orleans and off Brazos Santiago Pass for Zachary Taylor's Point Isabel garrison during the Mexican–American War, the brothers' sailing experience put them to work lightering war matériel ashore. They stayed aboard those ships through the war's end in 1848, then settled in Point Isabel for good — Albert opened a general store, Peter ran a hotel, and Nicholas built the sail lighters that gave the family its start ferrying goods off the bar.
Andrew's actual fateNot Lima — the 1849 California Gold Rush. "Despite the earnest entreaties of his brothers," Andrew sailed for California by way of Panama, crossing the isthmus overland. Letters arrived for a while, "and then suddenly, silence, and all efforts to reach him failed." This supersedes this page's earlier reading of the Status Animarum "Dimora" column as "Lima" — worth rechecking that word against the original image with this in mind.
The Solís match, in fullThe Solís family — Francisco Solís and Anastacia Rivas, with daughters Felicitas, Estéfana, and Teresa — fled the lawlessness of post-war Matamoros for Point Isabel at almost the same moment. Peter married Felicitas (1849/50), Albert married Estéfana (1850), Nicholas married Teresa, and later the brothers' nephew George (Simon's son) married a fourth Solís woman, Cirilda — resolving the "not yet placed" Cirilda Solís Champion from the Find a Grave data above. Joseph, arriving last, married Marcela Garcia, a Solís cousin through her mother Antonia — "the union... was now a reality, five-fold."
The ranches, and a surviving brandAlbert developed La Florida (Cameron County's oldest ranch settlement) and La Gloria, adjoining Josiah Turner's Galveston Ranch, plus range at Tío Caño. His cattle brand — a capital "A" whose crossbar extends into a "C," read as "AC Connected" — was still on cattle the author knew in the 1930s, and the branding irons themselves were still in the family, on the author's own wall, as of 2008.
A named family archiveA footnote cites "the Champion-Murphy family papers" directly, alongside a 1944 U.S. Government Printing Office volume, Texas Cattle Claims — both real, specific sources for anyone continuing this research, and a strong sign that Frank Champion Murphy or his descendants may hold considerably more than what's in this one article.
Who Frank Champion Murphy wasTraceable independently: born Vinton, Iowa, 1920, to Lucile Champion and Capt. Edward A. Murphy; spent boyhood summers on the family's La Gloria/La Florida ranch near Brownsville; became the American Angus Association's staff artist for nearly 60 years — his painting of the first Angus bull imported to the U.S. appeared on an 8-cent postage stamp in 1973, and over 130 of his works are held at Angus Association headquarters in St. Joseph, Missouri. He died 28 June 2010 in Wheaton, Illinois, survived by his wife Evelyn, son Tom Murphy, and daughter Julie (Rich) Heller — the two most concrete living-descendant leads found anywhere on this page, and the people most likely to know where the "Champion-Murphy family papers" are today.

The same author's companion piece, "Lucile Champion's Brownsville: Remembrances of Another Time" (also freely downloadable, same series, vol. 10), corroborates all of the above independently and adds Frank Champion Murphy's own line down from Albert: Albert's son Frank Champion married Henrietta Elizabeth Edwards — a Georgia family, Huguenot on her mother's side, that had resettled on the Mexican side of the border in Camargo and Mier after the Civil War — on 18 Aug 1886 at the Starr County courthouse, later solemnized as a Catholic ceremony in Brownsville. Their daughter Lucile Elizabeth Champion (b. 25 Feb 1889, Mier) was the author's mother. Henrietta and Lucile's baby sister Gussie both died of typhoid in 1894, three weeks apart, leaving Frank a widower raising five-year-old Lucile alone — Frank never remarried. Albert himself died in Brownsville in 1890, independently matching the Handbook of Texas date above.

Found: the primary source itself — and the author's own family

More pages have surfaced, from a spiral-bound book titled simply "Champion: The Champion Family of Point Isabel — Campeoni, Originally from Rovigno d'Istria, Italy." This appears to be the actual primary document both the Handbook of Texas entries and Frank Champion Murphy's academic article drew from — its "Part Three," on Albert, matches the Handbook almost line for line, and both are credited "By: A.A. Champion."

The author's own family, namedA group photograph captioned "A.A. Champion Family" names, directly: Mrs. A.A. "Chavela" Crixell Champion (A.A.'s own wife) and five children — Rodolfo "Rudy" Champion, Albert A. Champion Jr., Mary "Chickie" Champion Henggeler, Emile O. Champion, and John G. Champion II. The book's bibliography separately states the underlying family documents are "in possession of A.A. Champion, Brownsville, Texas." Between this family and Frank Champion Murphy's, and the "Rovigno Champions" Find a Grave project, this page now has three independent living-family threads to pull on — this one names the most people at once.
A.A. Champion, identifiedFind a Grave confirms it: "A.A. Champion" is Alberto Alfonso Champion (27 Jun 1899 – 21 Dec 1990, Port Isabel/Brownsville), "Chavela" is Isabel Crixell Champion (1900–2002) — "Chavela" being a common nickname for Isabel — and his own parents were John G. Champion (1862–1943) and Enriqueta "Henrietta" Orive Champion (1872–1912). That traces A.A. Champion's own line back through Joseph, the youngest of the original emigrant brothers, not Albert — a different branch than Frank Champion Murphy's.
His own children, mostly already gone tooOf the five children in the photo, two are independently confirmed deceased: Mary Champion Henggeler (1922–2005, no children shown) and Emile Oswald Champion (1924–2007, a WWII veteran whose own headstone reads "loving husband, father and grandfather" — so his line continues, just not yet traced). Rudy, Albert Jr., and John G. II haven't been checked individually. Any living contact from this branch is very likely a generation further down — grandchildren, most plausibly through the Henggeler or Babick (Emile's wife's family) surnames — which is a reasonable thing to ask "Rovigno Champions" about directly, rather than something to hunt for name-by-name here.

Simon's Rovigno/Pola line may still exist, in Croatia/ItalyThis book adds that Simon Apollonio's son Antonio (by his second wife, Eufemia Paliaga) married Antonia Cralich and died in Pola (Pula) on 27 Oct 1906; his brother Giuseppe and one of Antonio's own sons were still living in Rovigno shortly before World War II. That's a real, dated lead for descendants who never left Istria at all — worth mentioning specifically in the Centro di Ricerche Storiche letter.
A discrepancy worth naming plainlyThis book's own family list gives Giorgio and Maria Bronzin exactly six children — all sons, and never mentions a daughter. That conflicts with the typed "Apollonio detto Campione" chart used earlier on this page, which includes a daughter, Domenica (b. 1820). Two family-compiled sources disagree here; this page isn't resolving it in either direction, just flagging it for whoever can check an actual register.
George's birthdate — even this book disagrees with itselfThe narrative text states nephew George was "born in Rovigno on August 26, 1840" — matching Geni and this page's own 1860 census reading — but this same book's own genealogy card for George gives his birth as August 26, 1848. Same day and month, different decade, within one document. 1840 is very likely correct.

Color, for the record: this book preserves an actual quoted 1859 letter from Albert, refusing to run his stage line's mail into a Cortina-surrounded Brownsville — "I shall send the mail tomorrow, since it is pretty certain that the rider will be taken... I shall wait until I can send it safely..." — and confirms Albert's ranches, La Florida and La Gloria, and that "a member of the family together with the late Judge James B. Wells owned the entire [Point Isabel] town site at one point in time."

Where to look next

FamilySearch's microfilmed Rovinj parish collection (Matična knjiga, 1553–1913) only covers births, marriages, and deaths (Rođeni / Vjenčani / Umrli) — it does not include Status Animarum books, so this document was never going to turn up in an ordinary FamilySearch search. But the State Archive in Pazin's own register database (dapa.hr) lists the Status Animarum ("stanje duša") holdings for Rovinj/Rovigno directly:

Rovinj/Rovigno stanje dušaTen volumes, all held at ŽU Rovinj (the Rovinj parish office) and all marked as digitized and viewable in DAPA's reading room: 1861–1866, two undated "XIX. st." (19th century) volumes, two more spanning "XIX–XX. st.," then 1905–1920, 1908–1923, 1914–1923, 1923, 1923–1942, and 1928.
Best candidateHouse 833's own annotation is dated close to Giorgio's 1858 death, which is earlier than the 1861–1866 volume's stated range — Status Animarum books were running ledgers kept open for years, so the same physical volume very plausibly starts earlier than its catalogued end-date range suggests. The two 19th-century volumes marked only "XIX. st." are worth checking first alongside 1861–1866.
How to actually see themThese are marked "digitalno (u čitaonici)" — digitized, but viewable only in DAPA's own reading room in Pazin, not online. The practical path is a direct research request to Državni arhiv u Pazinu (contact via dapa.hr) asking specifically for the Status Animarum entries of Simon Apollonio (son of Giorgio, grandson of Simone) and of Pietro Apollonio (son of Simone, via widow Gaspera Segalla) in the Rovinj "stanje duša" volumes — the same request that, if it can reference the 1994 Radossi letter and house 833 as a known match, gives the archive a very specific thing to look for rather than a blind search.

Research roadmap — telling the fuller story

Finding house 833 answers the original question. These are the directions worth pursuing next, organized by what each one actually adds to the story rather than just closing the one open citation.

1 · Close the paper trail — the two missing annotations

Go straight to the sourceThe Centro di Ricerche Storiche di Rovigno (crsrv.org) — the institute Radossi directed from 1969 until 2018, and the one that supplied the 1994 letter and photo copies in the first place — is very much still open, at Trg Matteotti 13, Rovinj (info@crsrv.org, +385 52 811 133). Radossi himself is now honorary president of the Unione Italiana; the institute may still hold his 1994 research file on the Apollonio family, which would skip re-finding the other two annotations from scratch.
The archival fallbackIf the CRS's own file can't be found, the direct request to Državni arhiv u Pazinu for the Simon Apollonio and Pietro Apollonio/Gaspera Segalla Status Animarum entries (detailed above) is the fallback path.
Look for the letter itselfA surviving copy of the actual 1994 letter (to "A. Champion") — in family papers on the Texas side — would likely settle both a. what house numbers the other two annotations are under, and b. who "A. Champion" was, a living link between this research and whoever holds the rest of the family history book today.

2 · Why "Campione"? — the nickname's own origin

Read past the one quoted pageThe book quotes two local histories — Bernardo Benussi's Storia Documentata di Rovigno and Franco Stener's Rovigno d'Istria (1997) — but only for the Apollonio surname's own formation (from Francesco "da Brivoni," son of Polonio, arriving in Rovigno in 1522). Neither excerpt yet explains what made a later Apollonio a "champion" of something. Worth reading both in full for a passage that does.
Date the nickname more preciselyRadossi's letter says only that Simone (the earliest Apollonio named on the chart, or possibly his own father, unstated) carried it first, and that where and why is unknown. Since FamilySearch's own Rovinj microfilms (Rođeni 1560–1863, Vjenčani 1564–1907) are the town's actual parish registers — a different, earlier-starting set than the Status Animarum books — checking Apollonio entries there before 1749 (Simon's birth) could catch the nickname's first written appearance and possibly the occasion behind it.

3 · Why Texas, and why so early? — answered

Fully explained, by a descendant's own accountFrank Champion Murphy's 2008 article (see "Found: the origin story," above) answers this outright: the brothers went to sea individually to dodge Austrian conscription after the 1815 annexation of Istria, converged on New Orleans by 1846, and were pulled to Point Isabel specifically because the U.S. Army needed experienced sailors to lighter war supplies ashore for Zachary Taylor's garrison during the Mexican–American War. They stayed once the war ended in 1848. Nothing about this was accidental or purely maritime-general — it was wartime demand for exactly their skill, arriving at exactly the right port at exactly the right time.
Still worth pursuingThe Louisiana passenger record (found separately, likely Albert's own 1850 arrival) — worth checking whether it fits as a return trip or a separate family member's crossing, now that the main arrival is dated to 1846 via New Orleans rather than a single 1850 voyage. Texas naturalization records for the four brothers, and the diocesan sacramental registers for the earliest Point Isabel/Brownsville parish (see the ready-to-send letter, below), remain open.

4 · The five brothers and their descendants today

Reconstruct each brother's own familyWives, children, and where each Champion line settled — via census and Cameron County vital and land records — both for its own sake and because a descendant somewhere is the most likely person to hold the rest of the family history book (only page 128 has been seen on this page so far) or a copy of the Radossi letter itself.
BurialsFind a Grave and Cameron County cemetery records for Albert, Peter, Andrew, Nicholas, and Joseph Champion — a fast way to locate the family's own later generations.
Where to start Of these, the highest-leverage single move is track 1's first line: writing to the Centro di Ricerche Storiche di Rovigno directly. They are the same institute that produced the original 1994 research, they're still operating from the same building, and a live archivist there may be able to answer in one reply what would otherwise take a formal request to a second, separate archive (DAPA) and a guess at which of ten uncatalogued-by-date volumes to search.
